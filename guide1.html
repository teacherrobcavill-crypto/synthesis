Context How Subtractive Synthesis Changed Music

Before turning a single dial, it helps to see why synthesisers mattered so much — and how their hardware design shaped entire musical styles from the 1960s to today.

In the 1950s, creating electronic music meant wiring up laboratory test oscillators, tape loops, and radio equipment — pioneering work done at the BBC Radiophonic Workshop (Delia Derbyshire) and IRCAM. Those early setups had no piano keyboards and took hours to program.

By the late 1960s and 1970s, engineers like Robert Moog and Alan Pearlman (ARP) combined oscillators, filters, and amplifiers into playable keyboard instruments using voltage control (VCO / VCF / VCA). For the first time, musicians could sculpt brand-new timbres in real time.

1970s • Monophonic Era

Prog Rock, Funk & Cosmic Electronic

Early portable synths such as the Minimoog (1970) were monophonic (one note at a time). Because players couldn't play chords, synths became iconic for punchy basslines and expressive lead solos — using pitch-bend wheels, glide (portamento), and vibrato to rival an electric guitar (Stevie Wonder, Kraftwerk, Pink Floyd).

Late 1970s–80s • Polyphonic Era

Synth-Pop, New Wave & 80s Game Audio

Polyphonic synths (Prophet-5, Roland Juno/Jupiter) unlocked full chords, lush string pads, and bright synth brass. Alongside MIDI (1981), drum machines (TR-808, LinnDrum), and 8-bit game sound chips (NES, Commodore 64), a single composer could write melody, harmony, bass, and drums using pure waveforms.

Late 1980s–90s • Club Culture

House, Techno & Hip-Hop

Producers pushed filters to extremes — cranking up resonance for squelchy acid basslines (Roland TB-303), sweeping low-pass filters to build dancefloor tension, and using high portamento synth leads in G-Funk hip-hop.

2000s–Today • Modern DAWs

The Universal Blueprint

Modern software synths (Logic's Retro Synth, Serum, Vital, Ableton Analog) still follow this exact architecture. Even early analogue quirks like slight tuning drift are intentionally recreated today because subtle detuning makes synths sound warm and thick.

Why Producers Adopted Subtractive Synths (Key Context for A-Level Essays)
  • Two creative goals: Emulate acoustic instruments (brass, strings, flutes) or invent completely futuristic, otherworldly timbres.
  • One musician, full band: Affordable synths and drum machines helped launch the home-studio revolution.
  • Timbre as a musical hook: Sweeping a filter cutoff or resonance became an expressive production technique in its own right.

Part 1 What Is Subtractive Synthesis? & Signal Flow

Every subtractive synthesiser — from a 1970 Minimoog to a modern DAW plugin — follows the same left-to-right journey.

The Sculptor Analogy: Think of subtractive synthesis like carving a statue out of marble. You start with a bright, harmonically rich block of raw sound (Oscillator), chisel away the frequencies you don't want (Filter), and control how loudly the note starts, holds, and fades out (Amplifier).

1. VCO (Sound Source)

Voltage Controlled Oscillator. Produces a continuous, pitched waveform. Its octave range is measured in feet (like organ pipes): 16' for deep bass, 8' for normal pitch, 4' for high leads.

2. VCF (Tone Sculptor)

Voltage Controlled Filter. Removes unwanted frequencies to shape timbre. A Low-Pass Filter lets low frequencies pass and cuts bright highs — turning a buzzy wave into a warm bass or mellow pad.

3. VCA (Volume Gate)

Voltage Controlled Amplifier. Oscillators never stop humming on their own. The VCA acts like an automatic volume fader, opening when you press a key and fading out when you release it.

What moves the controls automatically? Two modulators act like "invisible hands": an Envelope Generator (ADSR) creates a one-shot shape every time you press a key (e.g. a snappy pluck or slow fade-in), while an LFO creates a repeating back-and-forth motion (e.g. vibrato or a rhythmic filter wobble).

1. VCO (Voltage Controlled Oscillator): The sound source. Generates a continuous pitched waveform. Octave range is measured in feet (16' for bass, 8' normal pitch). Layering a slightly detuned second oscillator thickens the sound.
A-Level Exam Keyword: Harmonic Richness — subtractive filtering can only remove harmonics that already exist in the raw VCO waveform (so a Sine wave has no upper harmonics to filter).

Part 2 Waveforms & Harmonics Explained Simply

Why does a flute playing Middle C sound totally different to a trumpet playing the same Middle C? The answer is harmonics.

Almost every musical sound is built from a fundamental frequency (the main pitch you sing) stacked with quieter, higher frequencies called harmonics (or overtones). The blend of harmonics creates an instrument's tone colour (timbre): few harmonics sound smooth and mellow; many harmonics sound bright and buzzy.

The Golden Rule of Subtractive Synthesis: A filter can only subtract harmonics that are already present — it cannot invent new ones. That is why synth designers start with a rich Sawtooth or Square wave, and almost never try to filter a Sine wave.
Waveform Shape What's Inside (Harmonics) How It Sounds Classic Musical Uses
Sine Fundamental only — zero harmonics Pure, smooth, whistle or soft flute Deep 808-style sub-bass, pure test tones, smooth leads
Triangle Odd harmonics only, fading very quickly Soft, mellow, warmer than a sine Flutes, gentle 80s game bass (NES), rounded electric keys
Sawtooth All harmonics (odd & even) — the richest wave Bright, buzzy, rich, brassy 80s synth brass, detuned string pads, cutting leads, big bass
Square / Pulse Strong odd harmonics only (1st, 3rd, 5th...) Hollow, woody, punchy (nasal when narrow) Retro arcade leads, clarinet tones, chunky synth bass, sub-oscillators
White Noise Unpitched — all frequencies randomly at equal level Static hiss (untuned radio) Synthesised hi-hats & snare snap (with HPF), retro explosions & wind

A-Level Extended Response Practice (8 Marks)

Exam Question: Explain how a producer would program a punchy 1970s analogue synth bass on a subtractive synthesiser, referring to the VCO, VCF, and ADSR Envelopes. (8 marks — AO1/AO2/AO3)
Band 4 / A* Mark Scheme Breakdown (7–8 marks):
  • VCO (AO1/AO2): Select a harmonically rich Sawtooth (contains all odd & even harmonics) or Square wave set to 16' octave range, optionally adding a subtle -1 octave sub-oscillator or slight detune (5–10 cents) for analogue warmth.
  • VCF (AO2/AO3): Route into a Low-Pass Filter (typically 24 dB/octave Moog ladder slope) with a low static cutoff (e.g. 250–600 Hz) to remove harsh upper harmonics, plus moderate Resonance (Q) to emphasise the cutoff point and add nasal punch.
  • Filter Envelope (AO2/AO3): Apply positive Filter Envelope Amount with an instant Attack (~5–10 ms), short Decay (150–300 ms), and low Sustain so each note opens brightly on the transient and snaps quickly back to a warm fundamental.
  • VCA Envelope (AO1/AO2): Fast Attack for immediate transient punch, medium Sustain to hold bass weight, and short Release (100–200 ms) to prevent muddy overlap between bass notes.
