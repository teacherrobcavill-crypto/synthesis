Module 1 Historical Context: How Synthesisers Reshaped Music

~15 mins • 3 Micro-Loops

Read each short concept below and immediately confirm your understanding before moving to the next one. Self-check key points unlock once you write your own attempt.

1.1 What Is Synthesis & Voltage Control?

Tier 1 • Comp 2 Guide

Synthesis means creating musical sounds artificially using electronic waveforms, with no microphone or acoustic instrument. In the 1950s (BBC Radiophonic Workshop / IRCAM), pioneers spliced tape loops and laboratory oscillators without keyboards. By the late 1960s, Robert Moog and ARP introduced Voltage Control — using electrical voltages controlled by a piano keyboard to shape pitch, tone, and volume in real time.

Micro-Check 1.1 Write ≥ 20 chars
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: Synthesis creates sound artificially from electronic waveforms (no acoustic recording). Voltage control + keyboards allowed musicians to perform pitched, sculpted timbres live in real time instead of spending hours splicing tape loops.

1.2 Monophonic vs. Polyphonic & Portamento

Tier 1 • Pearson Spec p.49 Tier 2 • AOS 3

Monophonic synths (like the portable 1970 Minimoog) play one note at a time — making them iconic for punchy basslines and expressive single-note lead solos using pitch-bend wheels and portamento (glide) (sliding smoothly between pitches). Polyphonic synths (late 1970s–80s: Prophet-5, Roland Juno/Jupiter) play multiple notes at once, unlocking chords, lush string pads, and synth brass.

Micro-Check 1.2 Write ≥ 20 chars
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: Monophonic = 1 note at a time (basslines, lead solos); Polyphonic = multiple notes simultaneously (chords, pads, brass). Portamento (glide) smoothly slides pitch from one note to the next.

1.3 MIDI (1983) & Analogue Tuning Drift in Modern DAWs

Tier 2 • AOS 3 p.11–12

MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface, 1983) connects synths, drum machines, and computers using serial digital messages — never audio. Key MIDI data includes Note Number (pitch/octave, e.g. C3) and Velocity (how hard a key is hit, 1–127). Meanwhile, vintage analogue synths suffered slight tuning drift as circuits warmed up — an imperfection modern DAW soft synths (Retro Synth, Vital, Serum) deliberately recreate via subtle detuning for warmth.

Micro-Check 1.3 Write ≥ 20 chars
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: MIDI transmits serial digital control instructions (bits/bytes), not sound — including Note Number and Velocity (1–127). Subtle detuning mimics warm analogue drift, thickening sterile digital tones.

2.1 Subtractive Synthesis & Why Order Matters (VCO → VCF → VCA)

Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.728

Subtractive synthesis starts with a raw, bright block of sound (VCO), chisels away unwanted frequencies (VCF), and shapes loudness over time (VCA) — just like Michelangelo carving a statue out of marble. Audio must travel in series: VCO → VCF → VCA.

Micro-Check 2.1 Write ≥ 20 chars
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: VCO (Voltage-Controlled Oscillator) → VCF (Voltage-Controlled Filter) → VCA (Voltage-Controlled Amplifier). The VCO must generate the harmonically rich raw audio signal first so the VCF has frequencies to attenuate.

2.2 Why the VCA Is Essential (Continuous VCO Drone)

Tier 1 • Core Synth Architecture

An analogue VCO never turns off — it oscillates continuously while powered on. The VCA (Voltage-Controlled Amplifier) sits at the end of the chain acting as an automatic volume gate, staying closed (silent) until a key triggers the envelope to open it, then closing cleanly when you release the key.

Micro-Check 2.2 Write ≥ 20 chars
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: A non-stop continuous drone at full volume, because the VCO oscillates constantly and nothing would gate notes on and off.

2.3 Audio Signals vs. Modulators (Control Signals)

Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.738

Audio signals carry the sound you hear (VCO → VCF → VCA). Modulators (ADSR Envelopes and LFOs) are silent control signals that act like invisible hands turning knobs automatically — ADSR fires a one-shot shape per key press, while LFO cycles continuously.

Micro-Check 2.3 Write ≥ 20 chars
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: Audio signals are heard at the speaker; modulation signals silently automate synth parameters. An ADSR fires a one-shot contour per key press; an LFO repeats continuously in a cycle.

Module 3 Pitch, Frequency, Harmonics & The Golden Rule

~15 mins • 3 Micro-Loops

To understand why filters work, you first need to know what lives inside a musical waveform.

3.1 Periodic Waveforms, Cycles, Oscillograms & Frequency (Hz)

Tier 0 • Advanced Acoustics Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.723

Pitched sounds vibrate in a repeating pattern called a periodic waveform. One peak-and-trough repetition is a cycle; a graph of amplitude over time is an oscillogram. The number of cycles per second is Frequency (Hz) — more cycles/sec = higher pitch (human hearing: 20 Hz – 20 kHz).

Micro-Check 3.1 Write ≥ 20 chars
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: A definite pitch comes from a repeating (periodic) waveform pattern (random vibrations sound like unpitched noise). 440 Hz means 440 complete waveform cycles every second.

3.2 Fundamental Frequency, Harmonics & Overtones

Tier 0 • Advanced Acoustics Tier 2 • AOS 2 Numeracy

The lowest frequency in a periodic wave is the Fundamental Frequency (f / 1st Harmonic) — the main pitch you sing. Stacked above it are Harmonics at exact integer multiples (2f, 3f, 4f, 5f...), visible on a line spectrum (FFT) via Fourier analysis. Watch out: because the 1st harmonic IS the fundamental, the 2nd harmonic = 1st overtone!

Example (100 Hz fundamental): 1st Harmonic = 100 Hz • 2nd (1st overtone) = 200 Hz (+1 octave) • 3rd = 300 Hz • 4th = 400 Hz.
Micro-Check 3.2 (Numeracy) Write ≥ 20 chars
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: 2nd = 100 Hz, 3rd = 150 Hz, 4th = 200 Hz, 5th = 250 Hz. The 2nd harmonic (100 Hz) is the 1st overtone.

3.3 Timbre, Partials & The Golden Rule of Subtractive Synthesis

Tier 2 • AOS 2 s.2 & s.11

Timbre (tone colour) is the blend of the fundamental, harmonics, and any non-harmonic partials (non-mathematically related frequencies like breath hiss or white noise). The Golden Rule: A subtractive filter can only subtract harmonics already present in the oscillator — it can never invent new ones.

Micro-Check 3.3 Write ≥ 20 chars
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: A filter can only attenuate harmonics already in the waveform — it cannot create new ones. A Sine wave has only the fundamental (zero upper harmonics), so a filter has nothing to sculpt.

4.2 Octave Footage (16'/8'/4'), Detune (Cents) & Sub-Oscillator

Tier 1 • Spec p.49 Tier 3 • Logic p.300 & 310

Organ Footage: Longer organ pipes play deeper notes: 16' = 1 octave down (half Hz), 8' = normal pitch, 4' = 1 octave up (double Hz, f_octave = 2 × f). Detune (cents): 100 cents = 1 semitone; detuning a 2nd oscillator by 6–18 cents creates slow phase beating that thickens the timbre. Sub-Oscillator: Adds a simple wave locked 1 octave below for solid low-end weight.

🎧 Practical Check 4.2 — Test Footage, Detune & Sub-Osc Do synth steps + write
☐ 1. Switch Octave to 16' ☐ 2. Raise Detune ≥ 10 cents ☐ 3. Toggle Sub-Osc ON
Write your observation first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: 16' drops pitch 1 octave into bass register; Detune around 8–15 cents adds thick chorus-like warmth (while 30+ cents sounds out of tune/dissonant); Sub-Osc adds solid low-end fundamental weight 1 octave down.

5.1 Filter Types (LPF, HPF, BPF) & Cutoff Frequency (Hz)

Tier 1 • Comp 1 & 2 Tier 3 • Logic p.597

Cutoff Frequency (-3 dB point) sets where filtering starts. A Low-Pass Filter (LPF) passes lows below cutoff and cuts highs above it; a High-Pass Filter (HPF) passes highs above cutoff and cuts lows below it; a Band-Pass Filter (BPF) passes only a narrow band around cutoff (cutting both highs and lows).

🎧 Practical Check 5.1 — Sweep LPF on Sawtooth vs. Sine + Test HPF Do synth steps + write
☐ 1. Hold C & sweep LPF < 350 Hz on Sawtooth ☐ 2. Switch Source to Sine & sweep LPF ☐ 3. Switch Filter Type to HPF
Write your observation first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: (a) Upper harmonics were stripped away right-to-left, turning the buzzy sawtooth into a warm, muffled bass. (b) Sine has zero upper harmonics above the fundamental, so the LPF has nothing to cut until it reaches the fundamental itself. (c) HPF removed the warm low-end fundamental weight, leaving thin highs.

5.2 Filter Slope (LP12 vs. LP24 — Decibels per Octave)

Tier 1 • Comp 1 & 2 Tier 3 • Logic p.213

Filter Slope measures how steeply frequencies beyond cutoff are attenuated in dB/octave. 12 dB/oct (2-pole) is gentler and airier; 24 dB/oct (4-pole Moog ladder) cuts twice as steeply per octave for darker, warmer, tighter bass weight.

Filter Maths (Tier 1 Comp 2): Doubling frequency = +1 octave (f_octave = 2 × f). On an LP12 at 200 Hz, 400 Hz is cut by -12 dB and 800 Hz by -24 dB. On an LP24 at 200 Hz, 400 Hz is cut by -24 dB and 800 Hz by -48 dB.
🎧 Practical + Maths Check 5.2 — Compare 12 vs. 24 dB/oct Do synth step + write
☐ Toggle Slope between 12 & 24 dB/oct with LPF around 600 Hz
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: (a) 24 dB/oct drops twice as steeply past cutoff, cutting bright highs much more aggressively for a tighter, warmer tone. (b) 1,000 Hz (1 octave up) = -24 dB; 2,000 Hz (2 octaves up) = -48 dB.

5.3 Resonance (Q / Emphasis) & Self-Oscillation

Tier 1 • Comp 2 Guide

Resonance (Q / Emphasis) feeds filter output back into its input to create a narrow volume boost right at the cutoff frequency. Moderate Q adds nasal punch; high Q creates squelchy "acid" sweeps (TB-303). At extreme maximum on analogue synths, the filter enters self-oscillation — generating its own pure sine-like pitch at cutoff!

🎧 Practical Check 5.3 — Sweep a Resonant Peak Do synth step + write
☐ Raise Resonance (Q) ≥ 10.0 on Sawtooth & sweep Cutoff
Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: High Q lifts a sharp peak right at the cutoff knee; sweeping cutoff creates a whistling, squelchy, vocal acid sweep. Self-oscillation is when extreme resonance makes the filter oscillate on its own, producing a pure pitched sine-like tone.

6.1 Attack, Decay, Sustain (LEVEL %) & Release + Velocity

Tier 1 • Comp 2 & 4 Tier 3 • Logic p.346 & 736

An Amplitude Envelope plots Volume (Y-axis) against Time (X-axis): Attack = time to reach peak; Decay = time to fall from peak to Sustain; Sustain = the held volume level (%) while the key stays pressed; Release = time to fade to silence after lifting the key. (MIDI Velocity 1–127 scales how strongly the envelope responds to key-strike force.)

#1 Exam Trap: Attack, Decay, and Release are TIMES (s/ms). Sustain is a LEVEL (%) — never a time!
🎧 Practical Check 6.1 — Sculpt a Pluck & a Slow Pad Do synth steps + write

Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: (1) The note died out to silence even while held because Sustain = 0%. (2) Slow Attack faded the note in gently; long Release let the note linger after lifting the key. (3) Long release on bass makes consecutive low notes overlap into muddy rumble.

7.1 Filter Env Amount: Funk Bass Transient Snap vs. 80s Brass Bloom

Tier 1 • Comp 2 Guide Tier 3 • Logic p.311

Filter Envelope Amount (Punch) pushes the cutoff frequency upwards on each key press. Instant Attack + short Decay gives a punchy bass snap (bright transient settling to warm fundamental); a slower Attack (~0.08–0.20 s) makes the note start muffled and bloom brighter like acoustic brass.

Key Points: Raising Punch swept the LPF open on every key press; fast Attack + short Decay gave a snappy percussive bass transient settling back to warm bass, while slower Attack (~0.12 s) made brightness bloom open like 80s synth brass.

8.1 LFO Rate, Depth, Tempo Sync, Shapes & The 3 Destinations

Tier 1 • Comp 2 & Spec p.49 Tier 3 • Logic p.345, 609, 738

LFO Rate sets cycle speed (free Hz or Tempo Synced to BPM, e.g. 1/8 note); Depth sets sweep width. LFO → VCO Pitch = Vibrato; LFO → VCA Volume = Tremolo; LFO → VCF Cutoff = Filter Wobble / Auto-Wah. Sine/Triangle gives smooth vibrato; Square jumps abruptly between two values.

🎧 Practical Check 8.1 — Hear All 3 LFO Destinations Do synth steps + write

Write your answer first to unlock self-check.
Key Points: (1) Vibrato cyclically modulates pitch (VCO); Tremolo cyclically modulates volume (VCA); Filter Wobble cyclically sweeps brightness/cutoff (VCF). (2) Square LFO jumped abruptly between two distinct pitches like a trill/alarm instead of smooth vibrato.
