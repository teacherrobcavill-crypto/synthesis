Module 1 Historical Context: How Synthesisers Reshaped Music

Est. 15–20 mins

Before you touch a single knob, you need to understand what synthesis is, why musicians adopted synthesisers from the 1960s onwards, and the exact vocabulary used to describe them in A-Level Music Technology.

1.1 What Is Synthesis? (Artificial Sound Creation)

Tier 1 • Component 2 Study Guide

Definition: In Music Technology, synthesis means creating musical sounds artificially using electronic waveforms, without recording any acoustic instrument or microphone source. It is a compulsory element of A-Level Component 2 (Technology-Based Composition).

In the 1950s, pioneers at the BBC Radiophonic Workshop (such as Delia Derbyshire) and IRCAM had to splice magnetic tape loops and wire up laboratory test oscillators just to create a single melody. There were no piano keyboards attached, so programming even a few seconds of music took hours.

1.2 Voltage Control & Early Hardware (1960s–1970s)

Tier 2 • AOS 3 Overview

Definition: Voltage Control means using electrical voltages to automatically control a synthesiser's pitch, brightness, and loudness. Engineers like Robert Moog and Alan Pearlman (ARP) combined voltage-controlled oscillators, filters, and amplifiers with a standard piano keyboard.

Early 1960s modular systems (like the Moog Modular) were huge, expensive, fragile, and needed physical patch cords. In 1970, the Minimoog packed the most important modular stages into a compact, portable keyboard with no patch cords required — making synthesisers practical for live rock, funk, and prog bands (Pink Floyd, Stevie Wonder, Yes, Kraftwerk).

1.3 Monophonic vs. Polyphonic

Tier 1 • Pearson Spec p.49

Monophonic: A synthesiser that can only play one note at a time (one voice). Early 1970s keyboards like the Minimoog were monophonic, which is why they became famous for heavy basslines and expressive single-note lead solos (using pitch-bend wheels and portamento/glide — sliding smoothly from one note's pitch to the next).

Polyphonic: A synthesiser capable of playing multiple notes simultaneously (chords). Late-1970s and 1980s polyphonic synths — such as the Sequential Circuits Prophet-5, Roland Juno/Jupiter, and Yamaha CS-80 — unlocked lush string pads, sustained chord progressions, and bright 80s synth brass.

1.4 MIDI (1983), Drum Machines & Modern DAW Soft Synths

Tier 2 • AOS 3 Overview p.11–12

MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface): Introduced commercially in 1983, MIDI is a universal digital communication standard allowing synthesisers, drum machines (TR-808, LinnDrum), sequencers, and computers to talk to each other. MIDI does not carry audio — it sends serial digital instruction messages (bits and bytes), such as Note Number (pitch & octave, e.g. C3) and Velocity (how hard a key is struck, from 1 to 127).

Analogue Tuning Drift & DAW Soft Synths: Vintage analogue oscillators drifted slightly out of tune as their circuits warmed up. Modern DAW software synths (Logic Pro's Retro Synth, ES2, Vital, Serum) recreate classic subtractive architecture digitally — and producers still deliberately add tiny amounts of detuning because that slight drift makes synths sound warm and thick.

Module 2 Subtractive Synthesis & Audio Signal Flow (VCO → VCF → VCA)

Est. 15–20 mins

Every subtractive synthesiser — from a 1970 Minimoog to Logic's Retro Synth or Vital — follows one universal left-to-right blueprint. Let's break down every single block and why the order matters.

2.1 What Does "Subtractive" Mean?

Tier 3 • Logic Pro Instruments Guide p.728

Definition: Subtractive synthesis builds a musical timbre by starting with a raw, harmonically rich waveform and removing (subtracting) unwanted frequencies using a filter, then shaping its volume over time.

The Marble Sculptor Analogy: When Michelangelo was asked how he carved the statue of David out of a block of marble, he replied: "I just cut away everything that doesn't look like David." In subtractive synthesis, the Oscillator gives you the raw block of stone, the Filter is your chisel removing frequencies you don't want, and the Amplifier controls how loudly the finished sound appears and fades out.

2.2 Stage 1 — VCO (Voltage-Controlled Oscillator)

Tier 1 • Core Synth Architecture

Function: The sound source. An oscillator generates a continuous, repeating electrical waveform at a controlled pitch (frequency). Without a VCO, there is no audio signal at all.

Key fact: An analogue VCO never stops oscillating — it hums continuously inside the synthesiser as long as the power is on. That is why we need a VCA at the end of the chain to turn notes on and off cleanly.

2.3 Stage 2 — VCF (Voltage-Controlled Filter)

Tier 1 • Component 2 Study Guide

Function: The tone sculptor. The VCF receives the raw, bright waveform from the VCO and attenuates (turns down) selected frequency ranges to shape the timbre — for example, removing harsh high frequencies with a Low-Pass Filter to turn a buzzy sawtooth into a warm bass.

Why must the VCF sit after the VCO? Because the VCO must generate the harmonically rich raw sound first before the filter has any frequencies to subtract.

2.4 Stage 3 — VCA (Voltage-Controlled Amplifier)

Tier 1 • Core Synth Architecture

Function: The automatic volume gate. The VCA sits at the end of the audio chain and controls the overall output amplitude (loudness) of the filtered signal. When you press a key, an envelope opens the VCA; when you release the key, the VCA closes so the note fades back to silence.

2.5 Audio Path vs. Modulators (Control Signals)

Tier 3 • Logic Pro Instruments Guide p.738

A synthesiser has two completely different types of signals flowing inside it:

  • Audio Signal (Solid black arrows below): The actual sound you hear travelling in series: VCO → VCF → VCA → Speaker Out.
  • Control / Modulation Signals (Dashed arrows below): Invisible "hands" that move knobs automatically — you never hear them directly. The two main modulators are the ADSR Envelope Generator (a one-shot contour triggered once per key press) and the LFO (a repeating cyclic wave for vibrato or wobble).

1. VCO (Voltage-Controlled Oscillator): Generates the raw, continuous pitched waveform. Exam Keyword: Harmonic Richness.

Signal Flow & Stage Roles

(2) Because the VCO oscillates continuously while powered on; the VCA + Envelope opens volume on key press and closes it to silence on release. (3) Audio signals carry the actual sound you hear to the speakers; modulation/control signals are silent automation instructions that move synth parameters (pitch, cutoff, volume) automatically.

Module 3 Pitch, Frequency, Harmonics & Timbre (The Physics of Sound)

Est. 20 mins

Why does a flute playing Middle C sound completely different from a trumpet or a synth bass playing the exact same note? To master subtractive synthesis, you need to understand what lives inside a waveform.

3.1 Periodic Waveforms, Cycles, Oscillograms & Frequency (Hz)

Tier 0 • Advanced Acoustics Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.723

Periodic Waveform & Cycle: Sounds that have a clear, definite musical pitch vibrate in a repeating pattern called a periodic waveform. One complete peak-and-trough repetition is called a cycle. A graph showing a waveform's amplitude (vertical axis) against time (horizontal axis) is called an oscillogram (what you see on an oscilloscope).

Frequency (Hz): The number of complete cycles that occur every second, measured in Hertz (Hz). Faster cycles = higher frequency = higher perceived pitch. Human hearing spans roughly 20 Hz (deepest sub-bass) to 20,000 Hz / 20 kHz (highest sparkle).

3.2 Fundamental Frequency, Harmonics & Overtones

Tier 0 • Advanced Acoustics Tier 2 • AOS 2 Numeracy Pack

Fundamental Frequency (f or 1st Harmonic): The lowest frequency in a periodic waveform. It is the main musical pitch your ear hears and sings (e.g. 100 Hz or Concert A 440 Hz).

Harmonics: Additional, higher frequencies stacked above the fundamental that occur at exact whole-number (integer) multiples of the fundamental: 2×f, 3×f, 4×f, 5×f... Using a mathematical process called Fourier Analysis, any complex repeating waveform can be displayed as a line spectrum (FFT graph) showing each harmonic bar individually.

Worked Example (AOS 2 Numeracy): If a note has a fundamental frequency of 100 Hz:
• 1st Harmonic (Fundamental): 1 × 100 Hz = 100 Hz
• 2nd Harmonic (1st Overtone): 2 × 100 Hz = 200 Hz (one octave above the fundamental!)
• 3rd Harmonic (2nd Overtone): 3 × 100 Hz = 300 Hz
• 4th Harmonic (3rd Overtone): 4 × 100 Hz = 400 Hz
• 5th Harmonic (4th Overtone): 5 × 100 Hz = 500 Hz
Watch Out for Overtone Numbering (Tier 0 Acoustics): The 1st harmonic IS the fundamental. Therefore, the 2nd harmonic is called the 1st overtone, the 3rd harmonic is the 2nd overtone, and so on!

3.3 Timbre, Partials & The Golden Rule of Subtractive Synthesis

Tier 2 • AOS 2 Numeracy Pack s.2 & s.11

Timbre (Tone Colour): The unique sonic character that separates a flute from a trumpet playing the same pitch. Timbre is created by the combination of the fundamental frequency, the strength and pattern of harmonics, and any unpitched partials (non-mathematically related frequencies, like breath noise, metallic clatter, or white noise).

The Golden Rule of Subtractive Synthesis: A filter can ONLY subtract harmonics that are already present in the oscillator waveform — it can never invent new ones. If you feed a pure sine wave (zero upper harmonics) into a low-pass filter, the filter has nothing to carve away!

(2) Harmonics are exact whole-number (integer) multiples of the fundamental frequency; non-harmonic partials are randomised or non-mathematically related frequencies (such as white noise hiss or distortion). (3) A filter can only subtract harmonics already present in the waveform — it cannot invent new ones. Because a sine wave has zero upper harmonics, a filter cannot turn it into a bright brass tone.

Module 4 Oscillator Waveforms & VCO Controls (Deep Dive + Live Lab)

Est. 25 mins

Every standard subtractive synthesiser offers five core oscillator waveforms plus pitch, octave footage, detune, and sub-oscillator controls. Read each explanation carefully, then test every single one with your own ears in the live Synth Lab.

4.1 The Five Classic Oscillator Waveforms

Tier 0 • Advanced Acoustics Tier 2 • AOS 2 Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.730–731
Waveform Shape Exact Harmonic Content Sound Character Classic Musical Uses
Sine Fundamental only (1st harmonic). Zero upper harmonics. Pure, smooth, whistle-like, zero buzz. Deep 808-style sub-bass, pure test tones, gentle flute tones.
Triangle Fundamental + odd harmonics only (3rd, 5th, 7th...), fading very quickly in amplitude. Soft, mellow, slightly warmer than a sine wave. Flutes, rounded electric keys, classic 8-bit NES basslines.
Sawtooth ALL harmonics — both odd and even (f, 2f, 3f, 4f, 5f...), decreasing steadily. Richest wave! Bright, buzzy, dense, brassy, cutting. 80s synth brass, detuned string pads, fat basses, cutting leads.
Square / Pulse Fundamental + strong odd harmonics only (1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th... e.g. 100, 300, 500, 700 Hz). Hollow, woody, punchy (becomes thinner/nasal as pulse width narrows). Clarinets/reeds, retro arcade leads, chunky synth bass, sub-oscillators.
White Noise Unpitched — completely random frequencies at equal average energy across the spectrum. (Pink noise emphasises lower frequencies.) Static hiss (like an untuned radio or waterfall). Synthesised hi-hats & snare snap (via HPF), wind, surf, risers & explosions.
Bonus Term — Pulse Width & PWM (Tier 3 Master Reference): A standard Square wave spends 50% of each cycle high and 50% low (a 50% duty cycle). Narrowing that pulse width makes the tone thinner and more nasal; modulating it continuously with an LFO is called Pulse Width Modulation (PWM), creating a thick, swirling chorus-like shimmer.

4.2 VCO Pitch & Layering Controls: Octave Footage, Detune & Sub-Oscillator

Tier 1 • Pearson Spec p.49 Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.300 & p.310

Octave Range in Feet (16', 8', 4'): Borrowed from pipe organ pipe lengths — the longer the pipe, the deeper the pitch! 8' is normal concert pitch, 16' is one octave lower (half the frequency, ideal for bass), and 4' is one octave higher (double the frequency, ideal for high leads). Remember the octave rule: f_octave = 2 × f.

Coarse Tuning vs. Fine Tuning (Detune in Cents): Coarse tuning shifts pitch in whole semitones (or octaves). Fine tuning / Detune shifts pitch by tiny fractions called cents (100 cents = 1 semitone). Detuning a second oscillator by 6–20 cents creates slow phase beating between the two waves, thickening and warming the sound dramatically.

Sub-Oscillator: A dedicated helper oscillator locked exactly 1 octave (or 2 octaves) below the main VCO (usually a simple square or sine wave at 50% duty cycle). It adds solid low-end weight and fundamental body without cluttering the bright upper harmonics.

Module 5 Filters (VCF): Types, Cutoff, Slope & Resonance

Est. 30 mins

The Voltage-Controlled Filter (VCF) is the heart of subtractive synthesis. Here we break down every single filter type and control so you can hear, see, and explain exactly what each one does.

5.1 Filter Types: Low-Pass (LPF), High-Pass (HPF) & Band-Pass (BPF)

Tier 1 • Component 1 & 2 Study Guides Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.597
  • Low-Pass Filter (LPF): Allows all frequencies below the cutoff frequency to pass unaffected, while attenuating (removing) high frequencies above the cutoff. It is the most common synth filter — turning bright, buzzy waveforms into warm basses, mellow pads, or smooth leads.
  • High-Pass Filter (HPF): The opposite of an LPF — allows frequencies above the cutoff to pass, while removing low frequencies below the cutoff. Essential for removing rumble or sculpting crisp hi-hats and snares from white noise.
  • Band-Pass Filter (BPF): Allows only a narrow band of frequencies centred around the cutoff frequency to pass, cutting both the lows below it and the highs above it (creating a hollow, telephone-like or vocal sound).

5.2 Cutoff Frequency (Hz)

Tier 1 • Component 2 Study Guide

Definition: The Cutoff Frequency (measured in Hz) sets the exact frequency threshold (technically the -3 dB point) where the filter begins to attenuate the signal.

On a Low-Pass Filter, turning the cutoff knob down (counter-clockwise, e.g. from 10 kHz down to 300 Hz) strips away more and more upper harmonics, making the sound darker and warmer. Turning it up lets more upper harmonics through, making the sound brighter and buzzier.

Everyday Analogy: Lowering a Low-Pass Cutoff is like slowly closing a heavy studio door on a bright synth speaker — the sharp treble fizz disappears first, leaving only the warm low bass thumping through the wood.

5.3 Filter Slope (dB/octave & Poles: LP6, LP12, LP24)

Tier 1 • Component 1 & 2 Study Guides Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.213

Definition: A filter never cuts frequencies off like a vertical brick wall — it rolls them off gradually. Filter Slope measures how steeply frequencies beyond the cutoff are reduced, expressed in decibels per octave (dB/oct). Filters are often labelled with two letters and a number, such as LP12 or LP24.

  • 6 dB/oct (1-pole, e.g. LP6): Very gentle roll-off — lets lots of upper harmonics leak through.
  • 12 dB/oct (2-pole, e.g. LP12): Medium, brighter, airy roll-off (classic Oberheim / Roland / Korg MS-20 feel).
  • 24 dB/oct (4-pole, e.g. LP24): Very steep, tight, warm roll-off — Robert Moog's signature 24 dB/oct transistor ladder filter, famed for thick, focused synth bass weight.
Worked Numeracy Example (Component 2 Study Guide): Remember that doubling a frequency raises pitch by one octave (f_octave = 2 × f).
Suppose an LPF is set to a cutoff of 200 Hz:
• With a 12 dB/oct (LP12) slope: at 400 Hz (1 octave above) the signal is attenuated by -12 dB; at 800 Hz (2 octaves above) it is attenuated by -24 dB.
• With a 24 dB/oct (LP24) slope: at 400 Hz it is already attenuated by -24 dB; at 800 Hz it is attenuated by -48 dB!

5.4 Resonance (Q / Emphasis) & Self-Oscillation

Tier 1 • Component 2 Study Guide

Definition: Resonance (also labelled Q, or Emphasis on a Minimoog) creates a narrow volume boost right at the cutoff frequency by feeding part of the filter's output back into its input.

  • Low/Zero Resonance (Q ≈ 0.7–1.5): Smooth, natural tone shaping with no peak.
  • Moderate Resonance (Q ≈ 3–8): Adds a distinct nasal punch, vocal edge, or focused presence right at the cutoff point.
  • High Resonance (Q ≈ 12–18+): Creates a sharp whistling, squelchy "acid" accent (iconic on the Roland TB-303). On analogue synths, pushing resonance to the extreme causes self-oscillation — the filter itself begins oscillating and generates its own pure sine-like pitch at the cutoff frequency!

Module 6 Amplitude Envelopes (VCA ADSR): Shaping Volume Over Time

Est. 20–25 mins

An oscillator + filter gives you a tone colour, but real instruments also have a distinct volume shape: a plucked guitar explodes instantly and dies away, while a bowed violin fades in gently. In synthesis, that volume shape is controlled by an ADSR Amplitude Envelope.

6.1 What Is an Envelope? (Axes & The 4 ADSR Stages)

Tier 1 • Component 2 & 4 Study Guides Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.736

Definition: An Amplitude Envelope is a graph showing how a note's loudness changes over time from the moment you press a key until after you let go. The horizontal X-axis measures Time (seconds or milliseconds) and the vertical Y-axis measures Amplitude/Volume (dB or %).

A — Attack (Time: ms / s)

The time it takes for the sound to rise from silence (0%) to its loudest peak (100%) once a key is pressed. Fast Attack (0.005 s) = instant percussive snap (piano, pluck, bass); Slow Attack (0.5–1.2 s) = gentle fade-in (bowed strings, ambient pad).

D — Decay (Time: ms / s)

The time it takes for the volume to fall from that initial 100% peak down to the Sustain level (while you are still holding the key). A short Decay with 0% Sustain makes a crisp pluck or drum hit.

S — Sustain (LEVEL %, NOT Time!)

The held volume level at which the note stays for as long as you keep holding the key down. 100% Sustain holds at full loudness (organ); 0% Sustain dies away completely even while you hold the key (plucked guitar / hi-hat).

R — Release (Time: ms / s)

The time it takes for the sound to fade from its current level back down to complete silence after you let go of the key. Short Release = tight, dry bassline; Long Release = dreamy, overlapping pad tail.

#1 First-Year Exam Trap (Tier 1 Component 4): Attack, Decay, and Release are all TIMES (seconds/ms). Sustain is a LEVEL (%) — never write "Sustain is how long the note lasts"!

6.2 Velocity Sensitivity

Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.311 & p.346

Definition: Velocity measures how hard a MIDI key is struck (1 = softest pianissimo, 127 = hardest fortissimo). When an envelope responds to velocity, playing softly produces a quieter (or darker) note, while striking firmly produces maximum level and punch.

Module 7 Filter Envelopes (VCF ADSR): Shaping Brightness Over Time

Est. 15–20 mins

An envelope doesn't only control loudness! Routing an envelope to the Filter Cutoff Frequency lets a note's brightness evolve automatically every time you press a key.

7.1 Why Route an Envelope to the Filter? (Filter Envelope Amount / Punch)

Tier 1 • Component 2 Study Guide Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.311

Definition: A Filter Envelope modulates the VCF's Cutoff Frequency over the duration of each note. The Filter Env Amount / Punch (Hz) control decides how far the envelope pushes the cutoff frequency upwards (positive modulation) from its static resting position.

Real acoustic instruments change timbre as they play:

  • Punchy Bass / Plucked String (Fast Attack + Short Decay): When you pluck a string, the initial transient is bright, then upper harmonics fade faster than the low fundamental. Setting a low static Cutoff (~350 Hz) + positive Filter Env Punch (~2500 Hz) + instant Attack + short Decay gives each bass note a bright "snap" that immediately settles into warm bass weight.
  • 80s Synth Brass Swell (Medium/Slow Attack): Acoustic brass starts mellow and blooms brighter as air pressure builds. Linking a Low-Pass Filter to a slower envelope Attack (~60–150 ms) causes the note to start muffled and bloom open into bright harmonics!

Module 8 LFO Modulation: Continuous Cyclic Movement

Est. 20–25 mins

An ADSR Envelope fires once per key press. What if you want a parameter to wobble, pulse, or shimmer continuously back and forth? That is the job of the LFO (Low-Frequency Oscillator).

8.1 What Is an LFO? (Rate, Depth, Sync & Waveforms)

Tier 1 • Component 2 Study Guide Tier 3 • Logic Pro Guide p.345, p.609, p.738

Definition: An LFO (Low-Frequency Oscillator) is an oscillator that vibrates below human hearing — typically between 0.1 Hz and 20 Hz (Component 2 highlights 0.5 Hz – 15 Hz). You never hear an LFO directly in the audio path; instead, it acts as an invisible hand automatically turning a knob back and forth in a repeating cycle.

  • LFO Rate (Hz) & Tempo Sync: Controls the speed of the repeating wave (e.g. 5 Hz = 5 cycles per second). Turning on Sync locks the LFO speed to the DAW project tempo (e.g. 1/4, 1/8, or 1/16 rhythmic pulses).
  • LFO Depth / Amount: Controls the intensity (width) of the movement — subtle depth adds gentle musical expression, extreme depth creates wild sirens or dubstep wobbles.
  • LFO Waveform Shape: Sine / Triangle = smooth, natural back-and-forth (ideal for vibrato); Square = jumps sharply between two values (e.g. trilling between two pitches); Sawtooth = rhythmic rising/falling ramp (bubbling / helicopter effects).

8.2 The Three Classic LFO Destinations (Must Know for Exams!)

Tier 1 • Pearson Spec p.49
LFO Destination What Is Modulated Musical Term / Effect Typical Musical Use
LFO → VCO Pitch Oscillator frequency goes slightly up & down Vibrato Expressive synth leads & strings (like a violinist rocking their finger; often controlled by the Mod Wheel)
LFO → VCA Volume Amplifier loudness pulses up & down Tremolo Retro electric piano shimmer, pulsing synth pads
LFO → VCF Cutoff Filter cutoff sweeps open & closed Filter Wobble / Auto-Wah Rhythmic EDM/dubstep bass wobbles, sweeping spacey pads
Exam Distinction: Never mix up Vibrato (pitch modulation: LFO → VCO) and Tremolo (volume modulation: LFO → VCA). And always distinguish LFO (repeating/cyclic) from ADSR Envelope (one-shot contour per note).

Band 4 / A* Mark Scheme Breakdown (7–8 Marks):
  • VCO (2 marks): Select a harmonically rich Sawtooth (all odd & even harmonics) or Square wave set to 16' octave range, optionally layering a slightly detuned 2nd oscillator (5–10 cents) or -1 octave sub-oscillator for warm low-end weight.
